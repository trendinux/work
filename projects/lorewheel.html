Lorewheel

Party games for one shared screen. A consumer product I designed end to end, from the first sketch to a paid checkout, now live at lorewheel.com.

Group games online usually ask every guest to download an app, make an account or join from their own phone before anyone has laughed once. Lorewheel starts from the opposite brief: one screen in the room, a host who presses play, and a game running in under a minute.

01
Discover

I mapped the occasions people actually gather for (a party, a family dinner, a team offsite, a wedding, Halloween) and the moment each one goes flat: the first half hour when people arrive in ones and twos and nobody knows what happens next. That moment, not the game itself, became the problem to design for.

02
Define

Four rules came out of it and every screen answers to them. The room is the user, so one screen is enough and phones are optional. No sign-up stands between a host and the first game. Names typed during a game stay on the page. And an event is paid for once, not by subscription.

03
Design

I designed the system first (navigation, the game card, the pack page, the checkout) and then gave each game its own look, type and illustration so a catalogue of 200 never reads as a template repeated. Design and front end moved together, so every decision was tried on a real screen the same day it was made.

OUTCOME

Live at lorewheel.com: more than 200 games, 56 of them free, eleven event packs sold once each, and a Pro plan for groups, with checkout through Stripe.

SELECTED SCREENS

From the first question to a paid pack

Six screens from the live product, captured from lorewheel.com: how a host finds a game, how two people play on one phone, how an event is sold, and what brings someone back between parties.

HOME
One question before a list of 200 games

A catalogue this size is a wall unless the first screen narrows it. The home page asks one thing, what are you planning?, and the four answers (party, family, team, game night) reorder everything below. The group sits in a panel on the right with example names that disappear the moment you add your own, so the page shows how the room will look before anyone types.

EVENT PACK · FRIGHT NIGHT
Sold for the night it's for, paid once

Packs are ten games for one kind of event: a wedding, a team offsite, Halloween. They are bought once and kept by the group, because nobody wants a subscription for a party they throw once a year. The page sells the evening rather than the list: what to play as people arrive, in the middle and to finish. The game and quiz pages offer whichever holiday is next on the calendar first.

LANDING · TEAM OFFSITE
A page for the person who books the offsite

The team pack has a different buyer: the person handed “can you sort out the offsite?” This page speaks to them, with an agenda, a facilitator's run-sheet and the end of the day (anonymous thanks, awards, a sealed note that opens at the next offsite). A free game sits beside the buy button so they can try it on their own before putting it in front of a team.

HEAD-TO-HEAD QUIZ · TWO PLAYERS
One phone, two players, facing each other

The quiz is played with a phone flat on the table between two people. This is the near player's half of the screen; the other half shows the same question turned around to face the player across the table, so each reads their own copy and has their own three buttons. The first right answer takes the point; a wrong answer locks that player out of the question. There is nothing to install and nothing to join: the device in the room is the whole product.

QUIZ SETUP
Two decisions, then play

Setup is two choices on one screen: who's playing (just me, against a computer that buzzes in after a few seconds and is sometimes wrong, or two players) and which quiz. Names typed here stay on the page. The page counts that a game started and finished, and nothing about who played.

DAILY
A reason to come back between parties

A party product is used a few times a year. Daily gives one person three quick games each day, the same three for everyone, so you can compare with friends. It is the habit loop for the weeks with no party in them, and it leads back to the group games.

ACROSS DEVICES

One responsive product on phone, tablet and desktop

Lorewheel is a website, not a store app, so every device runs the same code fitted to its screen. These are live screens captured from lorewheel.com: in Safari's engine at iPhone 15 and iPad Pro 11 size, in Chrome at Pixel 7 size and on a 1440-pixel desktop, shown in iOS, Android, iPad and browser frames.

IPHONE 15 · SAFARI · HOME
The desktop sidebar becomes five tabs

Below 760px the sidebar folds into a bottom bar with five tabs: Home, All games, Circle, Almanac and More. Everything else lives in the More panel, so a phone never has a sixth tab squeezed in. The planning question and the featured game stay above the fold.

IPHONE 15 · GAME PAGE
Key art first, one button below it

A game page on a phone is read by someone deciding whether to start it now. It leads with the game's own art, then its facts as tags (time, players, devices, price, age) and a full-width button that says exactly what happens. The rules sit lower, for the person who is still reading.

IPHONE 15 · BLIND RANK
A whole game inside one thumb's reach

Blind Rank is played alone on a phone. Each of the five slots is a full-width target, so a thumb can place an item anywhere on the list without stretching to the top of the screen. The item being ranked is the largest text on the page.

PIXEL 7 · CHROME · HOME
The same product at a taller ratio

Android phones are narrower and taller, and Chrome draws its own address bar. The order holds on both: the planning chips, the featured game, then the Most played row, with the five tabs fixed to the bottom where a thumb already is.

PIXEL 7 · QUIZ SETUP
Choices as large, square targets

Who is playing and which quiz are both picked from large tiles. The selected tile takes the accent outline, not just a colour change, so the choice still reads in sunlight and for people who can't tell the colours apart.

PIXEL 7 · DAILY
A daily habit, laid out like a card stack

Daily is the one part of Lorewheel built for a single person on a phone. Each of the three games is a full-width card with its own art and type, numbered one of three, so the day reads as a short stack to work through rather than a menu.

Past phone width there is room for the full sidebar again, so every section of the catalogue is one tap away and the five-tab bar is gone. The cover art fills the rest of the width, which is the point: on a tablet propped on a table, the games are the colour.

IPAD PRO 11 · QUIZ SETUP
A centred column, not a stretched one

The setup keeps a narrow centred column on a tablet instead of spreading the tiles across the screen. Two people sitting either side of a tablet both reach every choice, and each line stays short enough to read at a glance.

Desktop

DESKTOP · 1440 × 900 · QUIZ
Readable from across the room

Lorewheel is often a laptop plugged into the TV. On a 1440-pixel screen the quiz stays one centred card instead of stretching across the width, so on the television everyone looks at one place while the host works the laptop.

DESKTOP · GAME PAGE
The same order on every screen

The game page keeps the phone's order at desktop width: art, facts, one button, then the description. Nothing moves to a side column, so a host who found the game on a phone finds the button in the same place on the laptop.

Safe areas on both

The tab bar and full-screen games pad themselves by the phone's safe-area insets, so nothing sits under the Dynamic Island, the punch-hole camera, the home indicator or the gesture bar.

Installs like an app

The site ships a web manifest with standalone display and a theme colour that matches the frame, so “Add to Home Screen” on iOS and “Install app” on Android open it without browser chrome.

Set for readers past 40

The root is 18px and nothing is set under 14px, badges included. Tap targets are at least 44px, Apple's minimum, and the answer tiles and main buttons run well past it.

DESIGN SYSTEM

How I built Lorewheel from its design system

As the product designer I set Lorewheel up as two layers: a quiet chrome that every screen shares, and a separate art direction for every game. I then wrote the system down from the product’s own stylesheet, with a script reading every colour, size and radius out of the live CSS, so the documentation and the screens can’t drift apart.

  1. Decide the frame

    One committed dark theme, so the cover art carries the colour. An 18px root and nothing under 14px, because the host reading a phone at arm’s length is often past 40.

  2. Give each game a world

    Eight colour slots per game (stage, surface, ink, accent and the rest), plus its own medium, typeface and card object. A test fails the build if two games share a face, a card or a medium.

  3. Build parts from tokens

    Buttons, chips, tags and game cards read tokens, never hex values. The same tag sits on cover art and on a plain panel because its fill is a token, not a guess.

  4. Ship it to every screen

    The phone, tablet and desktop screens above are that one set of tokens at three widths. Then I handed the system over in the two places the next designer or agent will look for it.

ONE DECISION, TOKEN TO SCREEN
Press here is plum to violet

The primary button is six tokens and nothing else: two gradient stops, white text at 9.6:1, a 10px radius, the 800 weight and a violet glow. The component reads those tokens, and every screen that starts a game uses the component, so the live hero's “Play now” is the token decision, not a colour someone picked again.

GAME LOOKS · ONE MODE EACH
Ten worlds from one set of variables

Every look fills the same eight slots: stage, surface, ink, muted, accent, the ink on the accent, line and title. The card object differs per game (a Polaroid, a ticket stub, a diner receipt, a chalkboard), but the slots it reads from do not. That is what lets one engine render two hundred games and still give each its own face.

CHROME · ONE DARK MODE
Named for what they do

Chrome variables are named by role (surface, text, action) and point to primitives named by value. A designer picks text/secondary, not a grey, and a change to the grey reaches every screen.

TYPE · PLUS JAKARTA SANS
Eleven styles, one floor

Three weights and eleven text styles, with sizes bound to variables. The game title style is the one place a game's own typeface replaces the system face.

SHAPE, SPACE, DEPTH
Measured before it was handed over

Every colour pair was checked for contrast when the tokens were built. Primary and secondary text pass on every surface, and so does the ink on each game's card. Two findings went on the list to fix rather than into the file: the pink badge is 3.6:1 with white text, and six games' button accents fall under 4.5:1. The live site corrects those at runtime; the Figma file shows the raw values so a designer sees them.

WHAT THE AUDIT FOUND
Two buttons for one job

Writing the system down showed that the game pages use a different primary button from the app: the pink-to-orange brand gradient as a pill with dark text. It passes contrast (5.2:1 at its weakest), so it is a consistency problem rather than an accessibility one. I documented the app’s button as the standard and listed the game pages as the next change.

HANDED OVER
In Figma and in Claude Design

A Figma kit of layered boards with the tokens as variables, and a plugin that rebuilds the live pages as editable layers. The same system is set up in Claude Design (brand book, tokens, component previews), so an AI agent working on Lorewheel builds from it instead of from a screenshot.

A DETAIL WORTH DESIGNING

The way back from checkout

Browsers keep the page you just left in memory and hand it back exactly as it was, so a buyer who reached the payment page and pressed Back found the buy button frozen on “Opening checkout…”. Every page that opens checkout now resets its button when it is shown again. The person who comes back to think it over hasn't said no, and the screen shouldn't answer for them.
